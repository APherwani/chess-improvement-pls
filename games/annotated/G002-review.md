## Extended No-Engine Notes, Part 1

During the opening, I noticed that it was a little tricky for Black to protect the e5 pawn, so I played Re1. That naturally developed my rook on the open file and put a question to the pawn. If Black responded with ...f6, that would block my bishop and reinforce e5, but it would also weaken the light squares around Black's king and give me future ideas. If Black played what he did, ...Bd6, then there were other ways to keep being annoying.

On move 10, when Black played ...Bd6 to protect the pawn, I saw a good opportunity to develop my dark-square bishop with tempo by playing Bg5, questioning the queen. Again, Black could play ...f6 and weaken the light squares, or do something awkward, which he did with ...Nde7.

After that, I realized that if I took Black's light-square bishop, I would take away Black's possibility of creating a big center with ...f5 and would double Black's pawns. Also, the e5 pawn would become even more exposed, since I could play natural and sensible moves while Black had to play contortionist. The point was to create weaknesses and be annoying.

Then I developed my last piece: the knight went to d2 and then c4, questioning the e5 pawn. Meanwhile, Black needed to get out of a pin with a queen move. The whole idea won me a pawn, which was satisfying, and I was pretty sure I was better by move 13 or 14.

On move 16, Black played ...Qf6. The knight fork was poison because Black had a rook-and-queen battery charging at f2. Another problem I needed to solve was that my d3 pawn was underdefended in a practical sense. No one was attacking it yet, and it had two defenders, the knight and queen, but both were bad defenders: the knight could be challenged by Black's bishop, and my queen needed to move to connect the rooks.

Ideally, I wanted my queen on b3, targeting Black's weak e6 pawn and b7 pawn. The reason d3 was weak was that Black had a natural ...Rad8 move. If I tried to play d4, protected by c3, Black had the natural ...c5, and because my queen was on the d-file the whole thing looked like a jenga tower.

The move I was proud of was Re2. It solved two problems: most immediately, it protected f2; it also left the table open for Qb3 and allowed me to double rooks on the e-file. Black played ...Rad8 as predicted, but then I could move my queen to b3, because the knight was still protecting d3, and target Black's weaknesses.

## Extended No-Engine Notes, Part 2

On move 18, Black played ...Bd6 as predicted. Things were becoming tense, but by playing ...Bd6 Black blocked his rook, allowing my knight to move.

Here I had two good options. One was d4, which protects my knight. If things are exchanged, I take back with the pawn and attack Black's queen. That was straightforward enough.

I did not like d4 because there were too many possible shenanigans. Black might have ...c5, even if it may not work. Also, if Black trades his bishop for my knight, I have to recapture with the pawn because the rook is still defending f2. Then I cannot make an effective battery against Black's weak pawn on e6 anymore because my own pawn is in the way. Furthermore, I would give Black a great open d-file for his rooks, and I could not easily challenge it because my rooks were not connected.

So I looked for other options and found Ng4. This again threatened Black's queen and overdefended f2. Since the knight was out of the way and f2 was overdefended, Black's pawn weakness on e6 was very exposed: I had two attackers, queen and rook, while also making a double attack on Black's queen. I felt very proud of that.
